Job-market paper

Capitalizing the firm's human capital — an empirical analysis

Solo-authored · Job-market paper

Using Danish employer–employee data, I examine whether financial reporting is less informative about firm risk when firms rely more on human capital, and whether workforce information improves bankruptcy prediction.

Read abstract

Although firms often describe employees as their most valuable asset, financial reporting provides little consistent or comparable workforce information. This study investigates whether the lack of information concerning the firm's workforce implies that financial reporting is less informative for assessing firm risk when the firm is more dependent on labor, and whether enhanced human capital information in financial reporting can improve the prediction of firm survival. Using comprehensive Danish employer–employee registry data from 2002 to 2019, I develop and implement a model to empirically estimate firms' reliance on human capital. Then, I exploit a shock to lenders' information acquisition costs—the 2009 introduction of digital retrieval of financial reports from the Danish Business Authority’s central data repository—to assess whether the reduction in firms’ cost of debt associated with increased availability of financial reporting information is greater for firms that rely less on human capital. Consistent with my hypothesis, I find a significantly higher effect on the cost of debt for firms that are less dependent on human capital. Finally, I demonstrate that incorporating human capital information into accounting-based bankruptcy prediction models significantly improves out-of-sample predictive performance. Overall, the findings suggest that financial reporting is less informative for firms with a greater dependence on human capital, and that additional human capital information can meaningfully improve financial statement users’ ability to assess firm risk.

Working paper

Human capital investing and the role of mandatory human capital disclosures

Solo-authored

Do investors who advocate for human capital disclosure use that information? I study how the 2020 U.S. disclosure mandate relates to the portfolio allocations of Human Capital Management Coalition members.

Read abstract

This study examines the effects of mandatory human capital disclosure on the investment decisions of human capital-oriented investors. Specifically, I examine the effects of the 2020 U.S. human capital disclosure mandate on the portfolio allocations of members of the Human Capital Management Coalition (HCMC). As human capital disclosure has become an increasingly salient component of ESG reporting, this setting provides an opportunity to examine whether ESG-committed investors incorporate mandatory ESG disclosures into their investment processes, an issue on which prior evidence remains mixed. The results indicate that more informative human capital disclosures are associated with incrementally significant increases in HCMC members’ investments, whereas less informative disclosures are associated with incrementally significant decreases. In addition, post-mandate changes in HCMC members’ portfolio allocations are negatively associated with future workforce-related public enforcement actions, consistent with mandatory disclosure improving the alignment between investors’ ESG preferences and firms’ underlying workforce practices.

Working paper

Does the accessibility of financial reporting information affect firms' wage-setting power?

With Jeppe Christoffersen

A Danish reporting reform made company financials easier to access. Using matched employer–employee data, we find that the reform increased employee wages by approximately one percentage point, highlighting how financial information can shape labor market outcomes.

Read abstract

We examine whether increasing the accessibility of financial reporting information affects firms' wage-setting power. While a growing labor economics literature documents that firms possess substantial wage-setting power, much less is known about the factors that determine its extent. We explore whether employees' access to information about firms' financial performance is an overlooked determinant of firms' wage-setting power. We exploit a 2013 Danish mandate requiring limited liability firms to submit financial statements in XBRL format. Although the mandate did not expand firms' disclosure requirements, it substantially increased the accessibility of financial reporting information by enabling third-party intermediaries to collect, standardize, and disseminate firm-level financial reporting information at scale. Using population-wide matched employer-employee data and a difference-in-differences research design, we find that the mandate increased employee wages by approximately one percentage point. The effect is present for employees of both profitable and loss-making firms, is stronger in firms that rely more heavily on labor, and is accompanied by lower residual wages and reduced residual wage inequality. Our findings identify the accessibility of financial reporting information as a source of firms' wage-setting power and highlight a new channel through which financial reporting regulation affects non-investor stakeholders.
